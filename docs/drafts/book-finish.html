The finishing pass

An app can follow every rule in Books 20 and 21 and still feel unfinished. The difference is in the moments nobody draws: the screen with nothing on it yet, the two seconds of waiting, the message when something fails. This book is a pass over those, with a prompt for each.

Three screens nobody draws

A mockup shows a screen on its best day: full of tidy data, loaded, working. Real users meet it on its other days first. Book 15 gave you the names: the empty state, the loading state and the error state. A cheap app shows a blank page, a frozen page and a line of red code. A finished one has decided what each looks like.

StateWhenUnfinishedFinished
EmptyTeamDesk with no messages yetA white screen, or "No data"One sentence on what will appear here and one button to make the first thing
LoadingCorner Gym fetching today's classesNothing, then everything jumps into placeGray shapes where the classes will be, in the same layout, so nothing moves when they arrive
ErrorThe check-in didn't reach the server"Error: Failed to fetch""Couldn't check you in. Your phone seems to be offline." and a "Try again" button
Too muchA member named Maximiliano Fernández-Castellanos; forty classes in a dayText runs off the edge or pushes buttons off the screenLong text wraps or is cut with "…", long lists scroll, and the main button stays in reach

Anthropic's best-practices page shows how to ask. Its own example request reads: "build the settings page from the mockup. be ambitious: empty and error states, the save flow wired up, keyboard navigation, not just the layout". For screens that already exist:

States pass
For every screen in this app, show me four screenshots at 390px: with nothing saved yet, while it is waiting for data (hold the wait for 3 seconds so you can capture it), when the request fails, and with far too much (a 40-letter name, 60 items). Don't fix anything yet. Give me a table of what each one shows today and whether a user would know what to do next. Then propose the fixes, following DESIGN.md, smallest first.

Every tap answers back

A finished app never leaves you wondering whether your tap counted. How fast it must answer has been known for decades. Jakob Nielsen's summary of the research gives three limits that still hold:

TimeWhat the research saysWhat your app should do
0.1 second"about the limit for having the user feel that the system is reacting instantaneously"The button looks pressed the moment it is touched, before any answer comes back
1 second"about the limit for the user's flow of thought to stay uninterrupted"Anything slower shows that it is working: the button says "Checking in…", or the gray shapes from section 1 appear
10 seconds"about the limit for keeping the user's attention focused"Anything slower says how long it will take, and lets the person leave and come back

The result needs an answer too. When something is saved, say so, briefly, then get out of the way: a small "Saved" that fades, not a pop-up to dismiss.

Movement that helps

Book 20 said to remove animation that is only decoration. The movement worth keeping answers a tap and shows where something came from: a panel slides up from the button that opened it, a deleted card shrinks away. Keep it short. If you notice the animation itself, it is too long.

Some people get dizzy or sick from movement on screens, and phones have a setting for them. The web's reference for developers, MDN, describes how an app can "detect if a user has enabled a setting on their device to minimize the amount of non-essential motion". A finished app respects it.

Feedback and motion pass
Go through everything a user can tap in this app. For each, tell me: does it look pressed at once, what shows if the answer takes longer than a second, and what confirms it worked or failed. Give me a table and mark every gap. Then list every animation, how long it runs, and whether it answers a tap or is decoration. Propose: fixes for the gaps, removing the decoration, and turning all non-essential movement off when the phone's "reduce motion" setting is on (prefers-reduced-motion). Show me the list before changing anything.

The words

Words are the cheapest polish there is and the part Claude most often fills with filler. Nobody decided that the button says "Submit". Read every word in your app once, out loud, as if a friend were holding the phone.

RuleFillerWritten for this app
A button says what it does"Submit", "OK", "Continue""Check in", "Save card", "Feed Pip"
An error says what happened and what to do"Oops! Something went wrong""Couldn't save your card. Check your connection and try again."
It never blames the person"Invalid input""That date has already passed. Pick one from today on."
One name per thing, everywhere"deck", "set" and "collection" for the same thing"deck", every time
Numbers and dates the way people say them"2026-11-23T18:00", "1 classes""Today, 6 pm", "1 class"
No shouting, no cheering"AWESOME!!! You did it!""Checked in. See you at 6."
Words pass
List every piece of text a user can see in this app in one table: screen, where it appears, the words. Include buttons, empty screens, error messages, confirmations and anything shown only when something fails. Mark each one that is generic, blames the user, shows a technical message, or uses a different name for something named elsewhere. Propose a rewrite for each in plain, calm words. My users are [who they are] and they call this thing [their word for it]. Change nothing until I approve the table.

The table is worth having even if you change nothing. It is usually the first time anyone has seen all the app's words in one place.

Fonts, icons and pictures

Book 20's rule was one of each: one font family or two, one icon set, one kind of picture. This section is about where they come from, because every font, icon and photo was made by someone and comes with a license: the maker's rules for what you may do with it.

ThingWhat to look forFor example
IconsAn open license that allows any useLucide's license grants "permission to use, copy, modify, and/or distribute this software for any purpose with or without fee". Heroicons' page says: "This library is MIT licensed"
FontsThe license shown on the font's own page. "Free to download" and "free to put in an app" are different promisesHave Claude read the license and tell you in one sentence what it allows
Photos and drawingsWho made it, and whether you may use it in an app that others useYour own photos are the safest and usually the best
Pictures made with AIThe terms of the tool that made them, and one consistent styleFive images from five prompts look like five apps
The risky shortcut: putting a paid font or icon pack in the repo

You bought it, so it feels like yours to use anywhere, and committing the file makes the app work.

What it costs: in a public repo, anyone can download that file. You have handed out a product someone sells, which most paid licenses forbid. And as Book 3 explains, a file committed once stays in the repo's history after you delete it.

How to close it: for a public repo, use fonts and icons with open licenses. If a paid one matters to you, ask Claude what its license allows before it goes in: some may only be used from the seller's own service, some need a private repo. Keep a CREDITS.md file that lists every font, icon set and picture with its license and where it came from.

Asset check
List every font, icon, image, sound and video this app uses: the file or address, where it came from, and its license. For each, tell me in one plain sentence whether the license allows it in a public repo and in an app other people use, and quote the line that says so. Mark any you can't find a license for. Then write the list into CREDITS.md. Also tell me if the app mixes more than one icon set or more than one style of picture.

Two small pictures deserve a decision of their own: the app's icon and the preview that appears when someone shares its link. They are the first things a new user sees, and as Book 7 explains, an installed app's name and icon don't update by themselves. Get them right before you send the link around.

Test it where it lives

Screenshots are taken by a robot on a fast computer. Your users have a thumb, a bus, a cracked screen and one bar of signal. Before you call a screen finished, hold it.

  1. One hand. Can your thumb reach the main button without shifting your grip?
  2. Tap sizes. Google's guidance: "A minimum recommended touch target size is around 48 device independent pixels", with targets "spaced about 8 pixels apart". If you miss a button twice, it is too small or too close.
  3. Outside. Step into daylight. Pale gray text that passed on your sofa can vanish.
  4. Dark mode and big text. Switch the phone to dark, then set its text size to large. Nothing should disappear or overlap.
  5. Slow. Turn off wifi and try it on a weak signal. This is where the loading and error states from section 1 are seen for real.
  6. Someone else's phone. An older or smaller one than yours, if you can borrow it.

Two checks a robot can run

Google's PageSpeed Insights is a web page where you paste your app's address. Google says it "reports on the user experience of a page on both mobile and desktop devices, and provides suggestions on how that page may be improved." It gives three speed numbers, each with a target Google calls good:

NumberIn plain wordsGood
LCPHow long until the main thing on the screen has appearedWithin 2.5 seconds
INPHow long the app takes to react to a tap200 milliseconds (a fifth of a second) or less
CLSHow much things jump around while the page loads0.1 or less

The same page gives an accessibility score, from a tool called Lighthouse. Lighthouse's own description: the score "is a weighted average of all accessibility audits", and "each accessibility audit is pass or fail". It catches missing labels, low contrast and tap targets that are too small. A high score means the robot found nothing, not that a person using a screen reader will manage, so treat it as a floor.

Speed and accessibility check
I ran PageSpeed Insights on [address] for mobile. Here are the results: [paste, or attach a screenshot]. Explain in plain words what is slow and why, and what each accessibility finding means for a real user. Propose fixes in order of how much a user would notice, with the size of each change. Don't add any new package or outside service. After I approve and you've made the fixes, tell me which numbers I should expect to change when I run it again.

A fresh pair of eyes, and when to stop

Book 17 explained why the session that built something is the worst one to judge it. The same holds for looks. After a week on an app you stop seeing it. A fresh session sees it the way a new user does.

Finishing review
You did not build this app. Open it as a new user would, at 390px and at desktop width, in light and in dark mode, and take screenshots as you go. Change nothing. Use it for its main purpose from the first screen to the end, then report in plain English:
1. Any moment you didn't know what to do next, or whether a tap worked.
2. Any screen that is empty, waiting or failed without saying so.
3. Any text that is generic, technical or blames the user.
4. Anything that breaks DESIGN.md: a color, size, gap, corner or icon that isn't on its lists.
5. The three things a new user would notice first that make it feel unfinished.
Report only what a user would notice. Skip anything that is a matter of taste. Send me the screenshots with each problem marked.

Then show a person. Hand your phone to someone who has never seen the app, say nothing, and watch. Each time they hesitate, frown or ask a question, write it down. That list is worth more than any review a robot can run.

Straight talk

Polish has no natural end, and it is more pleasant than the work it replaces. Set the stopping point before you start: the checklist in Book 20 passes, the four states in section 1 exist for every screen, and one real person has used the app for its main purpose without asking you a question. Then stop and ship. As Book 23 says, a conversation with a real user beats a phase of polish.

Try it

Open one of your apps, turn on airplane mode, and tap its main button. Whatever you see is your error state. If it is a blank screen or a line of technical text, send the "States pass" prompt.
